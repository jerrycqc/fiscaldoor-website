A 409A that comes in higher than expected can sound like good news for about a day, until someone actually works through what it does to the option pool and to the board's confidence in the number behind it.

**Background**

The company had raised a Series A round about eighteen months earlier and was growing steadily, hiring roughly a dozen new employees a quarter across engineering and go-to-market. Every one of those hires received an option grant priced at the company's most recent 409A common-share fair market value, which meant the accuracy of that number mattered directly to dozens of individual compensation packages, not just to an abstract line item in a board deck.

**A valuation that didn't match the story**

The outside 409A provider returned a common-share valuation uncomfortably close to the Series A preferred price, a gap that should ordinarily be significantly wider given that preferred shares carry liquidation preferences, anti-dilution protection, and other rights that plain common stock does not. The board noticed the mismatch within a day of seeing the draft report, and the reaction was immediate concern rather than reassurance: if the 409A had drifted this far from what everyone understood the company's actual value progression to be, what else in the finance function's numbers might be off.

**Finding the actual cause, not just accepting the uncomfortable number**

Rather than treating the valuation as a fixed output to simply work around, the 409A provider's methodology was reviewed directly, line by line, against the assumptions it relied on. The real issue turned out to be the comparable-company set: it skewed toward larger, later-stage security vendors with meaningfully higher revenue, growth rates, and margin profiles than the company's actual stage warranted. That skew pulled the implied revenue multiple, and with it the entire valuation, upward in a way that had nothing to do with the company's own performance.

**Correcting the peer set and building the narrative**

The 409A provider was engaged directly to correct the comparable set to companies genuinely similar in stage, revenue scale, and growth rate, a legitimate and fairly common methodology adjustment rather than a request to simply hit a preferred number. Once the corrected valuation came back, a one-page memo was built connecting the corrected 409A, the Series A preferred price, and the company's actual growth trajectory into a single, internally consistent story, the kind a board member could repeat confidently and accurately to a future investor without having to caveat it.

**Why this kind of gap is more common than founders expect**

409A providers work from standardized comparable-company databases and valuation models that are not always recalibrated carefully for every individual client's specific stage and growth profile, especially at firms handling a high volume of reports on tight turnaround times. A comparable set that made sense for a client three tiers ahead in revenue can get reused, sometimes without a full re-screen, for a company at an earlier stage in the same broad sector. Catching that kind of drift requires someone reviewing the methodology itself, not just the headline number.

**Results**

The corrected 409A valuation came in 34% lower and back in line with the Series A preferred price band, preserving the option pool's intended value for the next two full hiring cycles and giving the board a valuation story that actually held together under scrutiny.
