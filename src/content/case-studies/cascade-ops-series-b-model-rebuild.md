A single flagged discrepancy, three weeks before a term sheet, is close to the worst time to discover that a financial model no longer reflects the business. Cascade Ops got that discovery anyway, and had five weeks to fix it.

**A model that drifted quietly, then loudly**

The Series A model had been built to a reasonable standard eighteen months earlier, and for a while it held up fine. But two pricing changes and a shift from seat-based to usage-based revenue each required a real rebuild of the underlying assumptions, not a patched formula, and neither happened. By the time Series B diligence started, the logo-count metric in the pitch deck no longer matched the customer list sitting in the data room, and a sharp-eyed associate found it within a week.

**Rebuilding from the general ledger, not the old spreadsheet**

Patching the existing model would have been faster, but it would have left the same structural problem in place: numbers that looked plausible without being traceable to a source document. We rebuilt the model from the general ledger up, reconciling every revenue line to actual invoiced usage and rebuilding the cohort retention analysis around the current pricing structure, so every figure in the new model could survive a direct question about where it came from.

**Rehearsing the hard questions before they were asked live**

Before the next investor call, we ran two mock diligence sessions with the CEO and Head of Sales, built around the kind of specific, slightly adversarial questions a real investment associate asks when they're testing whether a number holds up. Two of the questions we rehearsed came up almost verbatim in the real session that followed.

**Results**

The term sheet was signed five weeks later, at the top of the valuation range Cascade Ops had been modeling internally, with no further metric discrepancies raised during the remainder of diligence.
