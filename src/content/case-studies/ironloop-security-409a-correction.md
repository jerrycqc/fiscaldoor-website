A 409A that comes in too high sounds like good news until you notice what it actually does to your option pool, and to the board's confidence in the number.

**A valuation that didn't match the story**

Ironloop's outside 409A provider returned a common-share valuation uncomfortably close to the company's Series A preferred price, a gap that should normally be much wider given how preferred shares carry liquidation preferences and other rights that common stock does not. The board noticed immediately, and so would every new hire trying to understand what their option grant was actually worth.

**Finding the actual cause, not just the uncomfortable number**

Rather than treating the valuation as a fixed output to work around, we reviewed the 409A provider's methodology directly and found the real issue: the comparable-company set used in the analysis skewed toward larger, later-stage security vendors whose growth and margin profiles had little in common with Ironloop's actual revenue stage. That skew pulled the implied multiple, and the resulting valuation, upward.

**Correcting the peer set and building the narrative**

We worked with the 409A provider to correct the comparable set to companies genuinely similar to Ironloop's stage and business model, a legitimate methodology adjustment rather than a request to hit a target number. Once the corrected valuation came back, we built a one-page memo connecting the 409A, the Series A preferred price, and the company's real growth trajectory into a single story the board could repeat confidently to a future investor.

**Results**

The corrected 409A valuation came in 34% lower and back in line with the Series A preferred price band, preserving the option pool's intended value for the next two hiring cycles and giving the board a valuation story that actually held together.
