An accounting policy that has quietly been wrong for two years rarely announces itself. It usually takes one specific question from an outside party who has no reason to assume the existing approach is correct.

**Background**

The company sells implementation and subscription software to hospital systems and outpatient clinics, a customer base that tends to sign multi-year contracts bundling an upfront implementation services engagement with an ongoing subscription fee. For two years, the finance team, which had grown from one person to a small team over that period without ever fully revisiting its founding-era accounting choices, had recognized that combined revenue on a simplified straight-line basis across the life of each contract. It was a reasonable shortcut for a small team focused on cash and growth. It was not a shortcut built to survive a real audit.

**The conversation that changed the timeline**

An early scoping conversation with the company's incoming audit firm, standard practice ahead of any IPO-track engagement, made the gap explicit almost immediately: recognizing the services and subscription components separately, as the applicable accounting standard actually requires, would produce materially different revenue figures for prior periods, potentially shifting several quarters of recognized revenue by a meaningful percentage. Left uncorrected, that gap would have surfaced during fieldwork as a formal restatement, exactly the kind of finding that adds months to an audit timeline and invites broader scrutiny into every other accounting judgment the company had made.

**Rebuilding recognition before the audit firm arrived**

The revenue recognition policy was rebuilt from the ground up, working through the company's contract portfolio one agreement at a time to separate the implementation services and subscription components based on their actual standalone selling prices, then recalculating two full years of historical revenue under the corrected methodology, all completed before the audit firm's first day of fieldwork. Alongside that historical correction, the monthly close checklist and account reconciliation process were redesigned specifically to hold up under a genuine audit trail rather than the lighter internal review standard that had let the original issue persist undetected for two years.

**Why this particular mistake is common in B2B software selling to healthcare systems**

Contracts with hospital systems and large clinical groups are often negotiated as a single bundled price covering both a substantial implementation effort and an ongoing subscription, because that is how the buyer's procurement process prefers to see it. Translating that single negotiated price back into two separately recognized revenue streams, each on its own accounting schedule, is a step that is easy to skip under time pressure and easy for an audit firm to flag as soon as they look closely, which makes this exact gap unusually common among healthcare-focused B2B software companies heading toward their first real audit.

**What audit-readiness work actually involves, beyond fixing the one issue found**

Correcting the historical revenue recognition was necessary but not sufficient on its own. The broader audit-readiness engagement also involved walking through every other significant accounting policy, deferred revenue, capitalized software costs, accrued liabilities, with the same question in mind: would this hold up to an auditor who assumes nothing and verifies everything. Two smaller issues were found and corrected the same way, before they had a chance to become their own late discoveries during fieldwork.

**Results**

The audit-readiness review closed with zero material findings and no restatement required, keeping the company's IPO-track timeline intact and leaving it with a close process built to survive the deeper scrutiny still ahead as the actual IPO audit approaches.
